A watermark is the one limitation you cannot edit around. Length caps you can
work with, resolution caps you can live with, but a logo burned into the corner
means the recording is a draft no matter how good it is.

The good news is that plenty of Mac recorders never watermark anything. The
catch is that several of the best-known ones do, and they do it on exactly the
tier you would try first.

## What is a watermark on a screen recording?

A watermark is a logo or piece of text that recording software burns into your
exported video, usually to mark it as made on a free plan or during a trial.
Because it is part of the pixels rather than an overlay, it cannot be removed
afterwards without re-recording.

It is the one limitation you cannot work around. A length cap you can plan for
and a resolution cap you can live with, but a logo in the corner makes the
recording a draft no matter how good the rest of it is.

## 2. OBS Studio

![OBS Studio's website](/blog/tools/obs.webp)

Free, open source, and it has never watermarked anything. No time limit either,
and no account. If a clean file at no cost is the entire requirement, this is
the answer and nothing below it competes.

What it does not give you is the edit. There is no editor at all, so the zooms,
the cuts and the framing are a separate job in another app, which is the work
Prequel has already done by the time it opens.

**Watermark.** Never. **Price.** Free.

## 3. QuickTime Player

![QuickTime Player's support page](/blog/tools/quicktime.webp)

Already installed, never watermarks, and produces a clean MP4 in about four
clicks. For a quick clip where the video only has to be legible, this is a
perfectly good answer.

It records the screen or the camera, not both together, and it cannot capture
system audio without a virtual driver. There is no zoom, no editor beyond a
trim, and no way to make the recording look like anything other than what your
screen looked like.

**Watermark.** Never. **Price.** Free with macOS.

## 4. Cap

![Cap's website](/blog/tools/cap.webp)

Cap does not watermark, which makes it one of the better free options. It limits
you on length instead: the free tier stops recordings at **five minutes**, which
is fine for a bug report and not enough for a tutorial.

Past that it is $29 a year for a commercial desktop licence, worth reading
carefully next to Prequel's $29, since Cap's buys twelve months and Prequel's
buys the app outright with no cap on a take at any point.

**Watermark.** Never, but the free tier caps recordings at five minutes.
**Price.** Free, then $29/year.

## 5. Descript

![Descript's website](/blog/tools/descript.webp)

This is where it starts to bite. Descript's free tier **watermarks your exports
and caps them at 720p**, so anything you make while evaluating it is unusable
for publication. Editing video by editing the transcript is genuinely clever and
nothing else does it as well, but the free tier is a demo rather than a usable
plan.

Paid plans start around $16 a month billed annually, above Prequel's monthly
rate and well above its one-off, and your media lives on their servers as a
condition of the product working at all.

**Watermark.** Yes, on the free tier, along with a 720p cap.
**Price.** Free tier, then roughly $16 to $50 a month.

![Camtasia's website](/blog/tools/camtasia.webp)

It is also subscription only now, billed annually: TechSmith stopped selling

## 7. FocuSee

![FocuSee's website](/blog/tools/focusee.webp)

FocuSee's automatic zoom is good and it runs on Windows as well as macOS, but
its **free trial watermarks what it exports**, so you cannot evaluate it on real
work.

The lifetime comparison is the one worth doing: FocuSee's one-off licence is
$199.99 against Prequel's $29, and Prequel's fourteen-day trial produces clean,
publishable files rather than watermarked ones.

**Watermark.** Yes, during the trial. **Price.** $49.99/yr to $199.99 once.
