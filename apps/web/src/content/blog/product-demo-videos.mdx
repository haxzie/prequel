## What is a product demo video?

A product demo video is a short screen recording that shows somebody using your
software to get one specific job done. It is not a feature tour and not an ad.
It usually runs sixty to ninety seconds, sits on a landing page or in a launch
post, and its job is to replace the paragraph explaining what your product does.

What makes one good is mostly restraint: one job rather than every feature,
tight pacing with the slow parts cut out, and zooms that put the viewer's eye on
the thing being clicked. The recording itself is the easy part.

A product demo is the highest-leverage video most software teams make and the
one they put the least thought into. It goes on the landing page, into the
launch post, into the sales follow-up and into the onboarding email, and it is
usually a screen recording somebody made in one take on a Tuesday.

This guide is about the difference between that and a demo people finish
watching. Most of it is not about the recorder.

## Before you record

### Write the click path, not a script

Pick the one job the viewer wants done and record somebody doing it. Write down the clicks in order and rehearse them once, so the
recording does not contain you looking for a menu.

Anything you would have to apologise for on camera, fix before recording. Empty
states, a test account called "asdf", a notification from someone's group chat.

### Set the stage

Record at the resolution you will publish at, or above it. Scale the browser or
app up rather than down, since text that is comfortable on your display is small
on a phone. Close everything else and turn on Do Not Disturb.

Use a clean, plausible dataset. Real-looking names and numbers make a demo feel
like a product; `Lorem ipsum` and `test test` make it feel like a prototype.

### Decide about your face

A person in the corner materially increases how far people watch, especially in
a launch video. It also gets in the way during the dense parts. The right answer
is usually both: present for the introduction and the close, small or absent
during the detailed middle.

That is only possible if your recorder keeps the camera separate from the screen
rather than compositing it in while you record.

## The three things that make it look professional

### Zooms, on the thing you are doing

This is the single biggest difference between a screen recording and a product
video. Your viewer does not know where to look and your cursor is three pixels
wide. Pushing in on each action tells them where to look and hides the fact that
most of the screen is irrelevant to the current sentence.

Doing this by hand is where the afternoon goes: find the moment, set a keyframe,
set the scale, ease it, do it again forty times. Recorders that watch your
clicks and typing and place the zooms themselves remove essentially all of that
work.

[Screen recorders with automatic mouse zoom →](/blog/screen-recorder-mouse-zoom-mac)

### Pacing, which means cutting

Record loosely and cut hard. Every pause where nothing happens, every page load,
every moment you were reading rather than doing. A ninety-second demo that moves
beats a four-minute one that is complete.

### Sound that is not just your microphone

If the product makes a sound, record it. System audio on its own track lets you
duck the app's sound under your narration rather than choosing between them, and
it is the difference between a demo that feels like software and one that feels
like a slideshow.

## What to record with

Most tools in this category will capture a demo. Fewer will produce one.

- **Loom** is built for speed, not polish. Excellent for sending a colleague a
  walkthrough, wrong for a landing page. [The best Loom alternatives →](/blog/loom-alternatives-mac)
- **OBS Studio** captures anything at any quality, and leaves every edit to you.
- **Camtasia and ScreenFlow** are real editors where every zoom is a keyframe
  you place.
- **Screen Studio, FocuSee and Prequel** make the cinematic pass automatically.
