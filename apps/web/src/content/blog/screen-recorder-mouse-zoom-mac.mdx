A mouse zoom is the difference between a recording somebody watches and one they
squint at. Your screen is 3456 pixels wide and the button you are pointing at is
forty of them, so a full-screen recording asks the viewer to hunt for what
matters while you talk over it.

Automatic zoom fixes that by watching where you click and pushing in on it. Five
tools do this properly on a Mac. They differ in what triggers the zoom, how the
movement feels, and whether anything else in the frame moves with it.

## What is automatic mouse zoom?

Automatic mouse zoom is a recorder watching where you click and type while it
captures, then pushing the frame in on those moments when it opens the editor.
You get a video that moves with your attention instead of sitting at one
distance for its whole length, without placing a single keyframe.

The alternative is doing it by hand: finding each moment on the timeline,
setting a scale keyframe, easing it, and repeating that forty times. That is the
single biggest time cost in editing a screen recording, which is why this
feature defines the category.

## 1. Prequel

![The Prequel editor](/blog/tools/prequel.webp)

Prequel watches where you click **and what you type** while it records, then
opens the editor with the zooms already placed. Nothing to set up before you
record, and nothing to keyframe after.

The typing part matters more than it sounds. A click is one point in time, but
filling in a form or writing a line of code is a stretch of attention in one
place, and a recorder that only tracks clicks either sits wide through all of it
or snaps in and out on every field. Prequel treats a burst of typing as a moment
worth holding on.

What separates it from a plain zoom is what moves with it. The frame takes on a
perspective tilt so the push-in has direction rather than just scale, focus
falls away from whatever the zoom is aimed at, and the camera you framed after
the take stays put while the screen moves behind it. Every zoom is an ordinary
slice on the timeline, so you can retime it, point it somewhere else or delete
it.

Export runs to 4K at up to 120 fps, which is the frame rate that keeps a fast
push-in from strobing, and it renders on your own Mac with no upload.

**Price.** $29 once, or $9 a month. Fourteen days free.
**Requires.** An Apple Silicon Mac on macOS 14 or later.

## 2. Screen Studio

![Screen Studio's website](/blog/tools/screen-studio.webp)

Screen Studio invented automatic zoom on macOS and its cursor smoothing is
still the best-known version of the effect: the pointer glides rather than
jitters, and the zoom follows it.

It zooms on clicks rather than on typing, and it stops at scale. There is no
perspective tilt and no depth of field, so a push-in gets closer without the
frame gaining direction. It exports at 4K 60 where Prequel runs to 4K 120, which
shows up exactly during the fast zooms this whole category is built around.

On price, be careful which number you compare: $29 is the monthly rate and the
yearly one works out at $9 a month, the same as Prequel's monthly.

## 3. FocuSee

![FocuSee's website](/blog/tools/focusee.webp)

FocuSee does zoom-and-follow well and is the only tool here that also runs on
Windows, which makes it the answer if your team is split across both.

The pricing is the most complicated in this comparison, and the free trial
watermarks what it exports. The lifetime comparison is the sharp one: FocuSee's
one-off licence is $199.99 and Prequel's is $29, for the same category of
purchase, and Prequel's fourteen-day trial produces clean files rather than
watermarked ones.

**Price.** Standard at $49.99 a year or $24.99 a month for one machine, Advanced
at $79.99 a year for three, and an Advanced lifetime licence at $199.99 for five.

## 4. Cap

![Cap's website](/blog/tools/cap.webp)

Cap's Studio mode has automatic zoom alongside backgrounds, cursor controls and
camera layouts, and it is open source with a hosted option, which nothing else
here offers.

Its zoom is the plainest of the five: it scales, and that is it. Export tops out
at 4K 60, and the free tier caps recordings at five minutes, which rules out
most tutorials. Note that its $29 is a yearly commercial licence rather than a
one-off, so the same figure buys Cap for twelve months and buys Prequel outright.

**Price.** Free for personal use with a five-minute cap, $29/year for a desktop
licence, or $12 per user per month for Cap Pro.

## 5. Tella

![Tella's website](/blog/tools/tella.webp)

Tella records in the browser and hosts the result on a page you can brand. Its
zoom is preset-driven rather than automatic: you pick the framing, which is more
control and more work.

The frame rate is where it bites. Tella reserves 60 fps for its $19 Premium
tier, and a zoom at 30 fps judders in a way no amount of easing hides. Prequel
exports at up to 120 fps on every plan, including the $29 one-off.

**Price.** Pro at $13 per user per month and Premium at $19, with no free plan
and a seven-day trial.

## So which one

If you are on Windows or split across platforms, FocuSee. If you want an open
licence and a hosted link, Cap. If you record in a browser and want the video
hosted for you, Tella.

For a Mac, where the recording is going in front of people, Prequel:

**Tutorials and how-tos.** Zooms on typing as well as clicks, so a form or a
code edit is held rather than snapped through.

**Product tours and demos.** The tilt and the depth of field are what make a
push-in read as a product video rather than a magnifier, and there is no cap on
the length of a take.

**Social clips.** Every frame preset is in both plans, so a zoomed landscape
recording reframes vertical without losing the subject.

**YouTube.** 4K at up to 120 fps, which is the only frame rate here that keeps a
fast zoom clean, with no watermark on any plan.

$29 once against $199.99 for FocuSee's lifetime, or $9 a month against Screen
Studio's $29 monthly. Fourteen days free either way.
