## What is a screen recorder?

A screen recorder is software that captures what is happening on your display as
a video file, usually alongside your microphone, your webcam and the sound your
computer is producing. On a Mac it is how you make a tutorial, a bug report, a
product demo or a YouTube video without pointing a camera at your monitor.

Every screen recorder does the capture. What separates them is everything
around it: whether they get your Mac's own audio, whether they can put your face
on screen, whether they zoom in on what you are doing, and whether the result is
a file you own or a page somebody else hosts.

Recording your Mac's screen is easy. Recording something worth watching is not,
and the gap between the two is where most of an afternoon goes.

This is the guide we wish existed when we started building a screen recorder:
what macOS can and cannot do on its own, the four decisions that actually change
how a recording turns out, and where to go next depending on which of them you
are stuck on.

## What macOS gives you, and what it does not

Two things are built in, and both are more limited than people expect.

**QuickTime Player** records a screen or a camera. Not both at once, and not
into one file.
**The Cmd+Shift+5 toolbar** is the same engine with a lighter interface.

Neither captures **system audio**, so the sound coming out of your Mac never
reaches the file. Neither puts your **camera** over your screen. Neither zooms,
crops, trims beyond the ends, or does anything to make the result easier to
watch.

That is not a criticism. Half the recordings anybody makes should be exactly
this: press record, do the thing, send the file. The moment a recording is going
in front of people who did not ask for it, you need something else.

## The four decisions that matter

Almost every question about screen recording on a Mac reduces to one of these.

### 1. Is the sound in it?

System audio is the single most common thing people get wrong, because macOS
does not expose it through the old recording APIs. Modern recorders built on
**ScreenCaptureKit** get it directly. Everything else needs a virtual audio
driver such as BlackHole, and the setup is fiddly enough that most people give
up and re-record with the volume up in the room.

### 2. Is your face in it?

A recording with a person in it is watched further than one without. macOS
cannot do it at all, so this is always a third-party app, and the tools differ
in one important way: whether the camera is composited into the screen while you
record, or kept as its own file so you can move and resize it afterwards.

[Screen recorders for Mac with a webcam overlay →](/blog/screen-recorder-webcam-overlay-mac)

### 3. Can the viewer see what you are pointing at?

Your screen is 3456 pixels wide and the button you are clicking is forty of
them. A full-screen recording asks the viewer to hunt while you talk. Zooming
fixes it, and doing it by hand in a timeline is the single biggest time cost in
making a screen recording. Several tools now do it automatically by watching
where you click and type.

[Screen recorders with automatic mouse zoom →](/blog/screen-recorder-mouse-zoom-mac)

### 4. Is the file actually yours?

Some tools give you a file. Others give you a link to a page they host, and the
distinction only matters on the day you want to re-edit, re-upload, or leave.
Related: whether anything is stamped on the export.
