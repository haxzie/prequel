## What is Camtasia, and what is it good at?

Camtasia has been the default for training and tutorial video for two decades,
and its reputation is earned in one specific way: recording and editing live in
the same application, and a beginner can get a usable video out of it on the
first afternoon. Its highest review score, consistently, is ease of use.

It is also genuinely deep where it matters for courseware — a full multi-track
timeline, annotations and callouts, captions, quizzing, and SCORM output for an
LMS. It runs on Windows as well as macOS, with close feature parity now, and it
is Apple Silicon native.

If you are building an interactive course rather than a demo, nothing on this
page is a substitute for it.

## Why do people look for a Camtasia alternative?

Cost, and the shape of it.

TechSmith stopped selling perpetual licences on 1 January 2025 and moved to
subscription-only on 12 February 2025, billed annually with no monthly option.
People who had bought a licence outright, sometimes years earlier, found that
the model they had chosen no longer existed. The cheapest tier watermarks your
exports, and the AI features are reserved for the top tier. In mid-2026 the
legacy rates for long-standing customers were raised again.

The second reason is weight. Camtasia is a full video editor and it behaves like
one on a big project — the most repeated technical complaint is the timeline
becoming slow on long recordings, with multi-second stalls after each cut, and
crashes on heavy projects. TechSmith documents workarounds itself, including
keeping projects off cloud and network drives.

The third is that it does not do the automatic pass. Camtasia has zoom-and-pan,
but you place and time every one of them by hand on the timeline. For a
forty-minute course that is the work. For a two-minute demo it is more work than
the demo.

## Is Prequel a good Camtasia alternative?

For screen recordings that need to look edited, yes. For courseware, no.

Prequel watches where you click and type while it records and opens an editor
with the zooms already placed — the thing you would otherwise be keyframing —
each one carrying a perspective tilt and focus falling away from what it is
aimed at, so the push-in reads as a shot rather than a crop. The camera is
framed afterwards, the background is a setting rather than a compositing job,
the cursor is smoothed and hidden when it idles, and export runs on your Mac's
own media engine at up to 4K 120.

And nothing is held back by tier. Camtasia's cheapest tier stamps a watermark
on your exports until you move up, and reserves the AI features for the top one.
Prequel is $9 a month or $29 once, and both have everything in them — the zooms,
the perspective tilt, the focus falling away from the subject, 4K at 120 frames
per second, every preset, transcripts, no cap on a take and nothing watermarked
at any point. The two differ in how much you can keep on a shared link, and in
nothing that decides what your export is allowed to be.

## What do you give up by switching?

The editor's depth, and the course features entirely.

No quizzing, no SCORM, no captions workflow, no annotations and callouts
library, no multi-track compositing of arbitrary media. Prequel edits a screen
recording; Camtasia edits video. If your work is instructional design, that gap
is the whole job.

And Windows. Camtasia runs on both; Prequel is macOS on Apple Silicon.
