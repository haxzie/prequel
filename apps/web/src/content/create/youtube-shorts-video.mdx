Vertical video from a screen recording is usually a crop, and a crop of a
desktop is a bad video. The interface was laid out across a wide frame; taking
the middle ninth of it throws away most of what you were showing and puts the
webcam somewhere off the edge.

The alternative is recording everything twice, once per aspect ratio, which
nobody does after the first week.

## Framing that survives the switch

Prequel does not store the look in pixels. Every geometry setting — the camera
size, its corner, the padding, the radius — is a fraction of the frame's
shorter edge.

The practical effect is that switching a take from 16:9 to 9:16 keeps the
camera a quarter of the way across the frame rather than sliding it out of
shot, and keeps the padding proportional rather than enormous. The look holds.
You are re-framing the same take, not rebuilding it.

That invariant is enforced in the code that computes geometry, and the preview
and the exporter draw the same plan — so the vertical version you approve on
screen is the vertical version in the file.

## Which frames are there

Landscape 16:9, 4K, vertical 9:16, square 1:1 and portrait 4:5, plus named
presets for YouTube, Shorts, TikTok, Reels, X and LinkedIn.

Switching between them is a setting on the take, so one recording can produce
the long landscape cut and the vertical clip without a second capture.

## The zooms matter more here

Vertical makes a desktop recording smaller, not larger, so the pushes are doing
more work than they do in 16:9. Prequel places them from where you clicked and
typed during the take, and every one is an ordinary timeline slice you can
retime or aim somewhere else.

For a Short specifically, the useful move is usually a tighter level and a
faster speed than you would want in a long-form video. Both are per-zoom
settings.
