A product demo has a specific problem. The thing you are demonstrating is
usually small — a button, a field, a number that changes — and a raw screen
recording shows it at the same distance for the whole take. The viewer is
looking at a whole desktop while you talk about a corner of it.

The usual fix is an hour in an editor per minute of video, placing zooms by
hand and guessing where the interesting moment was. Most demos never get that
hour, which is why most demos look like a screen recording.

## The zooms are already placed

Prequel records where you click and where you type while it captures. By the
time the editor opens on your take, those moments have been clustered into
zooms — pushed in on what you were doing, pulled back out when you moved on.
You are not looking at raw footage waiting for work. You are looking at
something close to the finished video, deciding whether you agree with it.

Every one of those zooms is an ordinary slice on the timeline. Move it, retime
it, point it somewhere else, delete it. Nothing about the automatic pass is
baked in.

## Aim them at what matters

A demo usually needs two kinds of push. A cursor zoom follows the pointer
around the screen, which suits a walkthrough where you are moving between
parts of the interface. A region zoom holds still on an area you draw, which is
what a pricing table or a settings panel needs — a zoom that drifts about while
someone is reading is worse than no zoom at all.

Level, speed, tilt and yaw are set per zoom, and blur falls away progressively
from the focus, so a push reads as depth rather than a jump cut.

## The rest of it

The camera is captured as its own track rather than burned into the screen, so
whether it appears, what shape it is and where it sits are all still open when
the recording stops. Microphone and system audio are separate too, with
separate gains. Export is one MP4 at up to 4K, rendered on your Mac's own media
engine, with nothing uploaded anywhere to make it.
