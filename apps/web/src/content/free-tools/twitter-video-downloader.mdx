## Why the old links still resolve

A tweet from 2018 and a post from last week are the same record with the same
number on the end of the URL. Renaming the site did not renumber anything, so a
twitter.com link in an old bookmark folder, a Slack archive or a document
written years ago points exactly where it always did.

That matters here more than it sounds like it should. Most links people want to
save a video from are not fresh — they are something somebody sent a while ago
and is only now worth keeping.

## Reading a tweet without an account

This page asks for the tweet the way any site that embeds one does: the public
view, the same thing a logged-out visitor sees. No key, no login, nothing stored
about which tweets have been looked at.

The limit of that approach is the useful part of it. If a logged-out visitor
cannot see the tweet, neither can this page — so a protected account stays
protected, and the page says the tweet is unreachable instead of trying harder.

## Several files, not one

Twitter stores a tweet's video more than once, at different sizes, so it can
send a sensible one to a phone on a train and a sharp one to a desktop. Both are
still sitting there.

All of them are offered. Take the largest if the clip is going into something
you are editing. Take a smaller one if it is going into a slide or a message,
where a fifth of the size looks identical and sends instantly.

## The file is not re-encoded

What downloads is the file Twitter is serving, unchanged. Nothing is converted
on the way through, which is why there is no quality setting to get wrong, no
watermark, and no wait while something re-compresses.

## Where the rights sit

The file is yours to keep; the video is not yours to claim. Saving a clip to
watch later, to quote, or to reference in something you are making is ordinary.
Re-uploading somebody's work as though you made it is not, and that is true
whatever produced the file.

## If the video is one you are making

This page is for video that already exists somewhere. If you are recording it —
a product demo, a walkthrough, a bug worth showing someone — Prequel captures
your Mac's screen straight to MP4 at up to 4K and 120 fps, and hands you an
editor with the zooms already placed and the camera framed.
