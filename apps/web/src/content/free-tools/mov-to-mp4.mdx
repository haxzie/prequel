## Most MOV files do not need converting

They need rewrapping. QuickTime's container and MP4 can both hold H.264 video
and AAC audio, which is what a Mac screen recording and most iPhone videos
already are. So the streams are copied from one box into the other, byte for
byte, and the whole thing takes about a second with nothing lost and nothing
re-compressed.

When that is not possible, the page re-encodes instead. A ProRes master from an
editor, or HEVC from a recent iPhone, cannot go into an MP4 as it stands, so
every frame is decoded and encoded again as H.264 at CRF 23. That takes real
time and makes the file considerably smaller.

Both paths are tried in that order, automatically. You see which one ran on the
panel when it finishes.

## Why anybody needs this

Windows stopped shipping QuickTime in 2016, Android never had it, and plenty of
web apps reject .mov on sight. MP4 is the one video file you can send to anyone
without a conversation about which player they have.

## Nothing is uploaded

The conversion is ffmpeg compiled to WebAssembly, running in this tab on your
own processor. Your recording does not leave your machine, which matters when it
is an internal system, a customer's screen or an unreleased product.

## Recording to MP4 in the first place

QuickTime's own screen recorder writes MOV, which is why this page exists.
Prequel records your Mac's screen straight to MP4, H.264 or HEVC, at up to 4K
and 120 fps, and opens an editor on the take with the zooms already placed.
